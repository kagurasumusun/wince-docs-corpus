Landware

Industry:
Software developer

Profile:
New Jersey based LandWare develops, markets, and distributes personal productivity applications for the new wave of non-PC devices. As of August 1997, LandWare offers no less than six applications running under Microsoft® Windows® CE. In less than one year, sales of these applications constitute 33 percent of the three and a half year old LandWare's revenues.

The long run is what really interests Ken Landau. His firm, LandWare, has been developing, marketing, and distributing quality applications for handheld computing devices for three and a half years, but because the firm is looking five and 10 years into the future, LandWare decided early on to commit to developing applications for the Microsoft® Windows® CE platform. It's a commitment that has paid handsome dividends even in the very short run that the platform has had so far.

Investing in the Future
"We became interested when we heard that Microsoft was making an entry into this market with a product code-named Pegasus [the first Handheld PC]," Landau recalls. "My board of directors decided that if LandWare is going to continue to be a prominent player in the market then we needed to find out what Microsoft's plans were. I started making some calls on a Thursday to somebody I thought might be able to get us some information, and on Friday we got an invitation to attend the first developer's conference, which began the next Monday. I flew out to Washington that Saturday.

"There was no installed market at all at that time," Landau goes on to say, "but we decided that Microsoft had a pretty strong story and some pretty strong hardware players as part of the team. The longevity of the product looked like it would be sustained, so we got the development tools in house and started to work with it. It was really an investment in our future."

LandWare's first product for Windows CE, a financial calculator called the Financial Consultant, was running on Casio's Windows CE device on the day it was introduced at Comdex in the winter of 1996. Within the eight months that followed, LandWare brought to market no less than six personal productivity applications for Windows CE. During the same period, sales of LandWare's Windows CE-based applications climbed from zero to approximately 33 percent of the privately held firm's overall revenues.

"We're not retiring yet," quips Landau, "but then we didn't expect to. We're in this for the long haul. I can say that we're quite satisfied with sales to date and that we look forward to continued growth."

An Easy Transition to Windows CE
For LandWare, developing personal productivity applications for the Microsoft Windows CE environment was not a terribly complicated transition. The firm's lead programmers were already familiar with Microsoft Visual C++ from other development activities. They were also familiar with the principals of Object Oriented Programming and they had already been developing for the handheld marketplace.

"The advantage we had going into this," explains Landau, "was that we already knew the market. We understood the limitations of handheld devices and we knew what you could and couldn't, should and shouldn't do."

That notwithstanding, LandWare's developers would have faced greater challenges if they had had to develop without the well-designed development products that exist for the Windows CE environment, like Visual C++ for Windows CE.

"Visual C++ for Windows CE is very well done," agrees Landau. "Integration into VC++ and the Developer Studio IDE along with the object oriented development environment has lowered our development ramp-up time considerably. VC++ is an extremely robust development platform. We've been able to borrow code from other applications and use them in the new applications. We've had a couple of products that have used the same date picker, for instance."

Familiar APIs and Protocols
LandWare's applications run the gamut of personal productivity products. The firm's first application, the Financial Consultant, supports such complex calculations as earned, net present, and net future values, and interest rate calculations. Other applications include TakeNote, an "intelligent note pad" application, and Virtual Courier (developed by MicroBurst), which lets the user dial up and access their Microsoft Mail or CC:Mail remotely. Pocket Finance (developed by AnyWare) is a checkbook application for Windows CE that allows the user to interface with Microsoft Money or Intuit's Quicken on their desktop systems.

"You can't live on a handheld system alone," says Landau, "so we also develop small applications for the Microsoft Windows 95 and Windows NT workstation environments that let you upload and download information to your handheld system."

Developing both desktop and handheld applications, however, has been easy, given the high degree of integration between the Microsoft Windows CE and Windows 95 environments.

"We see Windows CE as a subset of the Windows 95 application programming interfaces (APIs) and protocols," says Landau, "so if you're familiar with Windows 95 it is easy to do this kind of development."

Key features of Visual C++ for Windows CE that LandWare developers have used include PC-based emulation of Windows CE devices, the registry editor, the fully optimized Windows CE cross-compilers, and remote debugging technology to complete their projects.

Devices for Everyday Use by Everyone
Because LandWare's foray into the Windows CE marketplace was an investment in its own future, and because the investment required was relatively low, the firm was willing to risk the startup costs of developing for the platform. " We were willing to bear that because LandWare is dedicated to this new market and we're taking a long term approach," says Landau. "A lot of people come to me and ask how we can be in a market where they're only going to sell hundreds of thousands when they're selling desktops in the area of 60 million per year. And my answer is, how big was the cellular phone market or the pager market 10 years ago?

"You have to ask yourself," Landau goes on to say, "do you believe in the market for non-PC devices? Do you believe that in five or 10 years everybody is going to be walking around with these devices in their pockets? LandWare thinks they will."
